# Word frequency lists

Published lists of how often words occur, which are free to use, and what each is good for in weaving. Looked up on 2026-11-21; check the licence again on the list's own page before copying a list anywhere.

## Why a frequency list

The words to weave first are the ones that return in every text. Counting one or two stories shows what those stories contain; a list counted from hundreds of millions of words shows what any text will contain. So the order in which a language's core words are woven is better taken from such a list than from the first story that happened to be chosen.

A count is a fact about a language and is not protected as such. A compiled list can be, which is why the licence matters.

## For the text being woven: English

| List                                           | Counted from                                      | Size                                | Licence |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------ |
| New General Service List (NGSL) 1.2            | 273 million words of the Cambridge English Corpus | about 2,800 words, with frequencies | CC BY-SA 4.0 |
| NGSL-Spoken                                    | spoken English                                    | about 720 words                     | CC BY-SA 4.0 |
| NGSL-Graded Reader                             | graded readers                                    | —                                   | CC BY-SA 4.0 |
| Wiktionary frequency lists (Project Gutenberg) | out-of-copyright books                            | —                                   | not checked |

The NGSL is by Charles Browne, Brent Culligan and Joseph Phillips, at <https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com>; the file with frequencies is "NGSL 1.2 stats". Its licence is the same as that of the texts in this repository. It counts by headword: go, goes, went and going are one entry, and he, him and his appear to be one as well. The sizes of the spoken and graded-reader lists are from memory or not looked up.

The NGSL is the list used here: see [`cebuano/weaving/WORD-ORDER.md`](../cebuano/weaving/WORD-ORDER.md) for its commonest words matched to the Cebuano core words.

## For the language being learned: Cebuano

| List                                                       | Counted from                                              | Licence |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Leipzig Corpora Collection, Cebuano (`ceb_community_2017`) | 11.5 million words, 2017, nearly all of Cebuano Wikipedia | the collection is CC BY 4.0; not confirmed for the Cebuano download |
| cebTenTen (Sketch Engine)                                  | 4.5 million words of web text                             | commercial, trial access |

<https://curl.corpora.uni-leipzig.de/languages/ceb>. A Cebuano list answers a different question: whether the core words chosen for the course are the frequent ones.

**The Leipzig word list is not a count of the language.** The file was opened on 2026-11-28. Of its 702,495 source pages, 701,547 are pages of the Cebuano Wikipedia, and nearly every sentence is of one pattern, a short article on a species: *kaliwatan* (species) and *gihulagway* (described) each occur about 690,000 times in 729,000 sentences. By recollection, most articles of the Cebuano Wikipedia were written by a program. So the commonest words of the file, after *ni*, *nga* and *sa*, are *maoy*, *kaliwatan*, *gihulagway*, *insekto*, and its word list should not be used as it stands.

A small part of the file is usable: 478 pages of the SunStar newspaper, 8,752 sentences and about 180,000 words of news and readers' letters. The file names the source of each sentence, so they can be taken out. That part was used on 2026-11-28 to check the tiers.

Whether cebTenTen has the same fault was not looked at. Both are written text, so the particles of speech (*na, pa, lang, gyud, ba*) will be counted too low in them.
