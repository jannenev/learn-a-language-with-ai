# What weaving is

A text that runs in one language and has words of a second language put into its sentences. What it is called, where it is found, what it can and cannot teach, and which kind this repository uses. How one is made here is in [`HOW-TO-WEAVE.md`](HOW-TO-WEAVE.md).

**Status.** Collected on 2026-11-21 by web search, with AI assistance. Almost nothing here was read in full: the sources were seen as abstracts or search summaries, and are listed at the end with that caveat. The reasoning about what fits this method is ours.

## Two things that look alike

|                                  | Made for learners                 | Written the way people talk |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Name                             | diglot weave; also sandwich story | code-switching; in the Philippines Taglish (Tagalog and English) and Bislish (Bisaya and English) |
| Who makes it                     | a course writer or publisher      | novelists, and everyone, in speech |
| Why the second language is there | to teach it                       | because that is how the readers speak |
| Does it have to sound natural    | no; it is a device                | yes; it is the natural thing |

**Diglot weave.** A story in the reader's language with words and phrases of the language being learned woven in, usually more of them as the story goes on. The reader follows the story because the sentence is in a language they know, and picks up the woven words from context and repetition. The term is credited to Robert Blair, who used it in the Power-Glide home study courses. Publishers selling books of this kind include Prismatext and One Third Stories. An earlier proposal of the same family is Robbins Burling's article of 1968, "Some outlandish proposals for the teaching of foreign languages".

**Taglish and Bislish.** Not a teaching method but a way of speaking and writing. Filipinos mix English into Tagalog or Bisaya sentences all day, and popular fiction is written that way: the Tagalog romance pocketbooks sold since the 1980s and the web novels printed from Wattpad are described as written in Filipino or in Taglish. Those are novels by real writers for entertainment, not learning material. A Tagalog or Bisaya reader meets English words in them inside sentences they understand, which is the same effect from the other side.

## What a weave can and cannot do

- **The story is never blocked.** The sentence frame is in a known language, so the share of unknown words can be set as low as the writer likes. A real story of any length can be read from the first day, which a text restricted to sixty known words cannot be.
- **The common words get many encounters.** The words a course teaches first are the frequent ones in any language (and, he, not, but, all), so one short tale repeats them dozens of times.
- **It teaches words, not sentences.** The grammar of the sentence stays that of the reader's language. A one-for-one swap also suggests that the two words match better than they do.
- **Some words cannot be woven at all.** Where the reader's language has a word and the other language has none (for Cebuano: "the", "is" and "was", the empty "it"), there is nothing to put in.
- **It cannot be listened to** as a sample of the language. Nobody speaks it.
- **Evidence is thin.** A thesis at Auckland compared mixed texts with graded readers for adults and states that nobody had tested the idea before; its result was not seen. Studies with small children are mixed.

## The kind used here

An English story, out of copyright, with the core words of the course woven in.

- **The words are the course's core words,** chosen by how often their English counterparts occur in the story, not by what the story is about. The aim is words that carry over to every text and to speech.
- **The same story is read several times, each time with more words.** Found when the first tale was read: the first version, with twelve words, took some looking up; the second, with twenty-one, read easily enough, because the story and the first twelve were already familiar; a third and a fourth were still readable with about a quarter of the words in Cebuano. This matches the repeated reading of the numbered texts.
- **A word is swapped only where the other language's word is right in that sense.** The rest stays English, and each woven story says in its notes what was left and why.
- **The two kinds of text do different work.** A woven story teaches words; the numbered texts teach the Cebuano sentence.

The other direction, a Cebuano sentence with English words dropped in, is how people in Cebu actually mix, and keeps the Cebuano grammar working. It has not been tried here.

## Weaving one's own book

Not tried. A learner could take an English book they would read anyway and have the words they know so far woven into it, at their own level.

- A plain script can do the swaps that have one meaning and one form (and, water, tomorrow). The rest needs judgment: word forms, and words where one language has one word and the other has two (the two Cebuano words for "not", "you" to one person or several). The workable division is an AI assistant doing the weaving from the reader's word list and the rules in `HOW-TO-WEAVE.md`, and a script checking the result.
- Changing a lawfully obtained copy of a book for one's own reading is allowed as private use in many countries, most of the EU among them, and the law differs by country; sharing a woven version of a book still in copyright is not allowed. A copy-protected e-book is a separate obstacle, and a shop's terms may forbid changing the file. This is not legal advice. This repository holds woven versions only of stories that are out of copyright.
