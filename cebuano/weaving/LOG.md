# Weaving Cebuano: what was tried, and what did not work

A log of the weaving of Cebuano stories, oldest first: what was tried, what happened, and what was changed. It is kept so that a later round, here or for another language, starts with what this one learned. The rules that came out of it are in `weaving/HOW-TO-WEAVE.md`; this file keeps the reasons and the dead ends.

So far: two Grimm tales and one reader. Each entry says whether its lesson looks general or belongs to Cebuano.

## 2026-11-21

**1. Which way round to mix.** First idea: an English sentence with Cebuano nouns in it. Judged too narrow at first, because the course's core is the small words, whose meaning lies in the Cebuano sentence; a Cebuano sentence with English words in it looked like the better fit. Looking at the core words one by one reversed that: most of them can stand in an English sentence as single words. *Lesson, general:* go through the word list before deciding what a method can carry.

**2. Nouns only.** A first plan allowed only nouns to come from one language into the other, since a verb needs its form. Dropped the same day: there is no reason to shut out verbs, pronouns or joining words, and they are the common ones. *General.*

**3. The first twelve words, chosen partly from the story.** The first version of *The Wolf and the Seven Little Kids* spent four of its twelve words on water, drink, sleep and food, which occur once or twice each. A second version replaced them with he and she, then, they and not; the reader preferred it. *Lesson, general:* count the text first, and take the words that are common in any text, not the ones the story is about.

**4. The same story again, with more words.** Not planned. The reader found the 12-word version took some looking up, and the 21-word version read easily enough: the story and the first words were familiar by then. Versions with 31 and 32 words were still readable. This became the design: one story at rising levels. *General, and it matches the repeated reading of the numbered texts.*

**5. A word is not always its dictionary partner.** "You will know him by his voice" could not take *kabalo*, which is knowing a fact, not a person. "Dear little mother" could not take *gamay*, which is small in size. "What" occurred nine times in *Little Redcap* and was a question only twice; the other seven were exclamations ("what big ears!"), which Cebuano says without its word for what. *Lesson, general:* look at every occurrence, and count the places where a word can be swapped, not the places where the English word stands.

**6. English helper words.** "Did not find" read badly as "did wala find", "wanted to go" as "gusto to moadto", "into the forest" as "sa the forest". The English helper was dropped in those places and nowhere else. *General in kind;* which helpers go depends on the language.

**7. One word where English has two.** *Siya* for he and she, and *iyang* for his and her, were expected to confuse and did not: the sentence told the reader who was meant. *Cebuano; other languages will have their own such words.*

**8. The place word, late and then early.** *Sa* stands for in, into, at, on and to. In the first tale it was held back to the last version, the 32nd word, and the reader was wary of it. In the second tale it came in the second version, among twenty-one words, and was "surprisingly easy". The likely reasons: most of the page was still English, the noun after it nearly always was, and the reader had just read the same places in English one version lower. *Lesson, probably general:* a hard word is easier early, with plain text around it, than late.

**9. The same word set for every story.** The second tale was first woven with the words of the first. It then brought the reader no new words, and words that happened to be rare in the first tale could never enter. Replaced by one order of words for all stories, from which each story takes the first N that occur in it. *General.*

**10. Our own texts as a measure of frequency.** A count of the numbered texts was offered as showing which core words are frequent. It cannot: those texts were written to use those words. A published frequency list of English was used instead. *General.*

**11. Plain swaps first.** A first order put all the plain swaps before all the ones that need judgment. That was the weaver's convenience: for the reader, *ikaw* where it stands simply means you. It also pushed very common words (you, not, that, we) behind rarer ones. Replaced by an order by how common a word is, counting only the places where it fits. *General.*

## 2026-11-22

**12. A word the sentences do not point to.** *Adlaw*, day, stood twice in the third version of *Little Redcap*: "Usa adlaw iyang mama niingon" and "so early sa adlaw nga ako shall still get didto". The reader understood every word around it and still had no idea what it was. Many words would fit either place. Taken out. *Lesson, probably general, from one reading:* a story suits a word when it has sentences that point to it, not merely when the word occurs. Whether words of time are weakly placed in most texts is not known.

**13. A form the language would not use there.** *Nakita*, saw, was put in to replace *adlaw*: five occurrences, clear sentences. But in a Cebuano sentence that form is built around the thing seen, and the one who sees takes another form of the pronoun (*nakita niya*, not *siya nakita*). "Siya nakita the sunbeams" pairs two words Cebuano would not pair so. Taken out the same day. *Lesson:* check not only the word but what it does to the words beside it. *The trap is Cebuano's;* any language whose verbs change the form of the words around them will have one like it.

**14. The number in the file name.** Versions are named by how many words they weave. In two days the third version of one story was 31, then 30, then 31, then 30. Not settled; naming by reading order (first, second, third) is the alternative.

**15. What a level is.** Not settled either. A step from one version to the next has several sizes: how many new words, how much more of the page changes, how hard the new words are in kind, and how well the story explains them. So far: about a dozen words and one word in seven at the first level, nine or ten more words and about five points more of the page at each step, one harder word per step.

**16. A story runs out of words.** After *adlaw* and *nakita* were taken out, nothing was left in *Little Redcap* to fill the thirty-first place: every other core word of Tiers 1 and 2 occurs once, or would change the sentence. A word that occurs once gives no repetition, so none was added and the third version stays at thirty. A story of about 1,400 words carried about thirty core words; more words need more stories, not more versions of one. *Lola*, grandmother, 23 times in this story, was considered and left in English: it is a word of this story, not one that returns in texts generally, and the reader needs it to follow who is who. (*Mama*, 13 times, was taken into the first tale's first version; whether a story's main noun should be woven is not settled.) *Probably general.*

## Slips in the making

Smaller, but each cost a correction; they are the reason for the checks in `weaving/HOW-TO-WEAVE.md`.

- Replacing "her" by *iyang* with a plain search also changed the "her" inside "grandmother".
- A swap inside a quotation dropped the comma that followed the word.
- English "I" is always a capital; *ako* is not, and came out as "Ako" in the middle of a sentence.
- "From the path into the wood" became *gikan sa path sa wood*: the same small word twice in a row with two meanings. One was put back into English.
- A story known to the reader was first counted as a flaw, since it cannot test the words. For a learner it is a help.
