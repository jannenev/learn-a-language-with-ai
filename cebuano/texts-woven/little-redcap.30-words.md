# Little Redcap

A woven story: the English tale with thirty Cebuano words and phrases put in place of their English ones. The third reading of this story: it keeps the twenty-one words of [the second reading](little-redcap.21-words.md) and adds nine. How to read a woven story: [`weaving/README.md`](../../weaving/README.md).

**Status.** The story is the Grimm tale in Margaret Hunt's translation of 1884, out of copyright; the original is [`weaving/originals/grimm-little-redcap.txt`](../../weaving/originals/grimm-little-redcap.txt). The weaving was done with AI assistance and has not been checked by a native speaker. It is a reading device, not a sample of how anyone speaks.

**How to read it.** Read the story as you would in English. Look through the words once before starting; do not memorise them. When one comes up and you have forgotten it, the sentence usually tells you; if not, look back here.

Once upon a time there was a dear gamay girl who was loved by everyone who looked at her, pero most of all by iyang grandmother, ug there was nothing nga siya would not have given sa bata. Once siya gave her a gamay cap of red velvet, which suited her so well nga siya would never wear anything else; so siya was always called “Little Redcap.”

Usa day iyang mama niingon to her, “Come, Little Redcap, diri is a piece of cake ug a bottle of wine; take them sa your grandmother, siya is ill ug weak, ug they will do her good. Set out before it gets hot, ug when ikaw are going, walk nicely ug quietly ug ayaw run off the path, or ikaw may fall ug break the bottle, ug unya your grandmother will get nothing; ug when ikaw go sa iyang room, ayaw forget to say, ‘Maayong buntag,’ ug ayaw peep sa every corner before ikaw do it.”

“Ako will take great care,” niingon Little Redcap sa iyang mama, ug gave iyang hand on it.

The grandmother lived out sa wood, half a league gikan sa village, ug just as Little Redcap entered the wood, a wolf met her. Redcap wala kabalo what a wicked creature siya was, ug was not at all afraid of him.

“Good day, Little Redcap,” niingon siya.

“Thank you kindly, wolf.”

“Whither away so early, Little Redcap?”

“Sa my grandmother’s.”

“Unsa have ikaw got sa your apron?”

“Cake ug wine; yesterday was baking-day, so poor sick grandmother is to have something good, to make her stronger.”

“Where does your grandmother live, Little Redcap?”

“A good quarter of a league farther on sa wood; iyang balay stands under the three dako oak-trees, the nut-trees are just below; ikaw surely must kabalo it,” replied Little Redcap.

The wolf thought to himself, “What a tender young creature! what a nice plump mouthful—siya will be better to eat than the old woman. Ako must act craftily, so as to catch both.” So siya walked for a short time by the side of Little Redcap, ug unya siya niingon, “See Little Redcap, how pretty the flowers are about diri—why do ikaw dili look round? Ako believe, too, nga ikaw dili hear how sweetly the gamay birds are singing; ikaw walk gravely along as if ikaw were going sa school, while everything else out diri sa wood is merry.”

Little Redcap raised iyang eyes, ug when siya saw the sunbeams dancing diri ug didto through the trees, ug pretty flowers growing everywhere, siya thought, “Suppose ako take grandmother a fresh nosegay; that would please her too. It is so early sa day nga ako shall still get didto in good time;” ug so siya ran gikan sa path into the wood to look for flowers. Ug whenever siya had picked one, siya fancied nga siya saw a still prettier one farther on, ug ran after it, ug so got deeper ug deeper sa wood.

Meanwhile the wolf ran straight sa grandmother’s balay ug knocked sa door.

“Who is there?”

“Little Redcap,” replied the wolf. “Siya is bringing cake ug wine; open the door.”

“Lift the latch,” called out the grandmother, “Ako am too weak, ug cannot get up.”

The wolf lifted the latch, the door flew open, ug without saying a word siya niadto straight sa grandmother’s bed, ug devoured her. Unya siya put on iyang clothes, dressed himself sa iyang cap, laid himself sa bed ug drew the curtains.

Little Redcap, however, had been running about picking flowers, ug when siya had gathered so many nga siya could carry no more, siya remembered iyang grandmother, ug set out on the way to her.

Siya was surprised to find the cottage-door standing open, ug when siya niadto sa room, siya had such a strange feeling nga siya niingon to herself, “Oh dear! how uneasy ako feel today, ug at other times ako like being with grandmother so much.” Siya called out, “Maayong buntag,” pero received wala answer; so siya niadto sa bed ug drew back the curtains. Didto lay iyang grandmother with iyang cap pulled far over iyang face, ug looking very strange.

“Oh! grandmother,” siya niingon, “what dako ears ikaw have!”

“The better to hear you with, my bata,” was the reply.

“Pero, grandmother, what dako eyes ikaw have!” siya niingon.

“The better to see you with, my dear.”

“Pero, grandmother, what dako hands ikaw have!”

“The better to hug you with.”

“Oh! pero, grandmother, what a terrible dako mouth ikaw have!”

“The better to kaon you with!”

Ug scarcely had the wolf said this, than with usa bound siya was out of bed ug swallowed up Redcap.

When the wolf had appeased iyang appetite, siya lay down again sa bed, fell asleep ug began to snore very loud. The huntsman was just passing the balay, ug thought to himself, “How the old woman is snoring! Ako must just see kung siya gusto anything.” So siya niadto sa room, ug when siya came sa bed, siya saw nga the wolf was lying in it. “Do ako find thee diri, ikaw old sinner!” niingon siya. “Ako have long sought thee!”

Unya just as siya was going to fire at him, it occurred to him nga the wolf might have devoured the grandmother, ug nga siya might still be saved, so siya wala fire, pero took a pair of scissors, ug began to cut open the stomach of the sleeping wolf. When siya had made two snips, siya saw the gamay Redcap shining, ug unya siya made two snips more, ug the gamay girl sprang out, crying, “Ah, how frightened ako have been! How dark it was inside the wolf;” ug after that the aged grandmother came out alive also, pero scarcely able to breathe. Redcap, however, quickly fetched great stones with which sila filled the wolf’s body, ug when siya awoke, siya gusto to run away, pero the stones were so heavy nga siya fell down at once, ug fell dead.

Unya all three were delighted. The huntsman drew off the wolf’s skin ug went home with it; the grandmother nikaon the cake ug drank the wine which Redcap had brought, ug revived, pero Redcap thought to herself, “As long as ako live, ako will never by myself leave the path, to run sa wood, when my mama has forbidden me to do so.”

It is also related nga once when Redcap was again taking cakes sa old grandmother, another wolf spoke to her, ug tried to entice her gikan sa path. Redcap, however, was on her guard, ug went straight forward on iyang way, ug told iyang grandmother nga siya had met the wolf, ug nga siya had said “maayong buntag” to her, pero with such a wicked look sa iyang eyes, nga kung sila had not been sa public road siya was certain siya would have eaten her up.

“Well,” niingon the grandmother, “we will shut the door, that siya may dili come in.”

Soon afterwards the wolf knocked, ug cried, “Open the door, grandmother, ako am little Redcap, ug am fetching you some cakes.” Pero sila wala speak, or open the door, so the greybeard stole twice or thrice round the balay, ug at last jumped sa roof, intending to wait until Redcap went home sa evening, ug unya to steal after her ug devour her sa darkness. Pero the grandmother saw unsa was sa iyang thoughts.

In front sa balay was a great stone trough, so siya niingon sa bata, “Take the pail, Redcap; ako made some sausages yesterday, so carry the water in which ako boiled them sa trough.” Redcap carried until the great trough was quite full. Unya the smell of the sausages reached the wolf, ug siya sniffed ug peeped down, ug at last stretched out iyang neck so far nga siya could no longer keep iyang footing ug began to slip, ug slipped down gikan sa roof straight sa great trough, ug was drowned. Pero Redcap went joyously home, ug never did anything to harm anyone.

## Notes on the weaving

- **Three words for not.** *Dili*, from the first reading: something is not being done. *Wala*: something did not happen, or is not there ("Redcap wala kabalo", "siya wala fire", "sila wala speak", "received wala answer"). *Ayaw*: an order not to do something, all three in the mother's warning ("ayaw run off the path", "ayaw forget", "ayaw peep"). Left in English where the choice is less plain: "would not have given", "was not at all afraid", "had not been", "cannot".
- **Iyang** is his and her before the thing owned, as *siya* is he and she: *iyang grandmother, iyang cap, iyang eyes*. *Sa iyang room*, in her room, is word for word how Cebuano says it. *Her* after a verb or small word stays English ("devoured her", "niingon to her").
- **Gikan** is from, and takes *sa* after it: *gikan sa village*, from the village. So *sa* comes four times more than in the second reading.
- **Maayong buntag,** good morning, is the first whole phrase woven: *maayo*, good, joined to *buntag*, morning. "Good day" stays English.
- **Unsa** is what, when asking: "Unsa have ikaw got sa your apron?", "saw unsa was sa iyang thoughts". "What dako ears ikaw have!" is an exclamation and keeps *what*; Cebuano says those another way.
- **Kaon** is eat, **nikaon** ate: "the better to kaon you with", "the grandmother nikaon the cake". "Would have eaten her up" and "better to eat than the old woman" stay English.
- **Kung** is if. "As if" stays English.
- **Mama** is mother.
- **Four more English helper words are gone:** "did" three times before *wala*, and "do" before *ayaw*.

The notes on the earlier words are in [the first](little-redcap.12-words.md) and [the second reading](little-redcap.21-words.md).
