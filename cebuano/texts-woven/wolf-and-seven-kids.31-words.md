# The Wolf ug the Seven Gamay Kids

A woven story: the English tale with thirty-one Cebuano words put in place of their English ones. The third reading of this story: it keeps the twenty-one words of [the 21-word version](wolf-and-seven-kids.21-words.md) and adds ten. How to read a woven story: [`weaving/README.md`](../../weaving/README.md).

**Status.** The story is the Grimm tale in Margaret Hunt's translation of 1884, out of copyright; the original is [`weaving/originals/grimm-wolf-and-seven-kids.txt`](../../weaving/originals/grimm-wolf-and-seven-kids.txt). The weaving was done with AI assistance and has not been checked by a native speaker. It is a reading device, not a sample of how anyone speaks.

**How to read it.** Read the story as you would in English. Look through the new words once before starting; do not memorise them. When one comes up and you have forgotten it, the sentence usually tells you; if not, look back here.

There was once upon a time an old goat who had seven gamay kids, ug loved them with tanan the love of a mama for iyang children. Usa day siya gusto moadto into the forest ug fetch some pagkaon. So siya called tanan seven to her ug niingon, “Dear children, ako have to moadto into the forest, be on your guard against the wolf; if siya come in, siya will devour you tanan—skin, hair, ug all. The wretch often disguises himself, pero kamo will know him at once by iyang rough voice ug iyang black feet.”

The kids niingon, “Dear mama, kami will take good care of ourselves; ikaw may go away without any anxiety.” Unya the old one bleated, ug went on iyang way with an easy mind.

It was dili long before someone knocked at the balay-door ug called, “Open the door, dear children; your mama naa diri, ug has brought something back with her for each of you.”

Pero the gamay kids kabalo nga it was the wolf, by the rough voice; “Kami will dili open the door,” cried sila, “ikaw art dili our mama. Siya has a soft, pleasant voice, pero thy voice is rough; ikaw art the wolf!”

Unya the wolf went away to a shopkeeper ug bought himself a great lump of chalk, ate this ug made iyang voice soft with it. Unya siya came back, knocked at the door of the balay, ug cried, “Open the door, dear children, your mama naa diri ug has brought something back with her for each of you.”

Pero the wolf had laid iyang black paws against the window, ug the children saw them ug cried, “Kami will dili open the door, our mama has wala black feet like thee; ikaw art the wolf.”

Unya the wolf ran to a baker ug niingon, “Ako have hurt my feet, rub some dough over them for me.” Ug when the baker had rubbed iyang feet over, siya ran to the miller ug niingon, “Strew some white meal over my feet for me.”

The miller thought to himself, “The wolf gusto to deceive someone,” ug refused; pero the wolf niingon, “If ikaw wilt dili do it, ako will devour thee.” Unya the miller was afraid, ug made iyang paws white for him. Truly men are like that.

So now the wretch niadto for the third time to the balay-door, knocked at it ug niingon, “Open the door for me, children, your dear little mama has come home, ug has brought every one of you something back from the forest with her.”

The gamay kids cried, “First show us thy paws that kami may kabalo if ikaw art our dear little mama.” Unya siya put iyang paws in through the window, ug when the kids saw nga they were white, sila believed nga tanan siya said was true, ug opened the door. Pero who should come in but the wolf! Sila were terrified ug gusto to hide themselves. Usa sprang under the table, the second into the bed, the third into the stove, the fourth into the kitchen, the fifth into the cupboard, the sixth under the washing-bowl, ug the seventh into the clock-case. Pero the wolf found them tanan, ug used no great ceremony; one after the other siya swallowed them down iyang throat. The youngest, who was in the clock-case, was the only one siya wala find. When the wolf had satisfied iyang appetite siya took himself off, laid himself down under a tree in the green meadow outside, ug began to tulog. Soon afterwards the old goat came home again from the forest. Ah! What a sight siya saw didto! The balay-door stood wide open. The table, chairs, ug benches were thrown down, the washing-bowl lay broken to pieces, ug the quilts ug pillows were pulled off the bed. Siya sought iyang children, pero sila were nowhere to be found. Siya called them one after another by name, pero no one answered.

At last, when siya came to the youngest, a soft voice cried, “Dear mama, ako am in the clock-case.” Siya took the kid out, ug it told her nga the wolf had come ug had eaten tanan the others. Then you may imagine how siya wept over iyang poor children.

At length in iyang grief siya went out, ug the youngest kid ran with her. When sila came to the meadow, didto lay the wolf by the tree ug snored so loud nga the branches shook. Siya looked at him on every side ug saw nga something was moving ug struggling in iyang gorged belly. “Ah, heavens,” niingon siya, “is it possible nga my poor children whom siya has swallowed down for iyang supper, can be still alive?” Unya the kid had to run home ug fetch scissors, ug a needle ug thread, ug the goat cut open the monster’s stomach, ug hardly had siya make usa cut, than usa gamay kid thrust its head out, ug when siya cut farther, tanan six sprang out one after another, ug were tanan still alive, ug had suffered wala injury whatever, for in iyang greediness the monster had swallowed them down whole. What rejoicing there was! Sila embraced their dear mama, ug jumped like a sailor at iyang wedding. The mama, however, niingon, “Now adto ug look for some dako stones, ug kita will fill the wicked beast’s stomach with them while siya is still tulog.” Unya the seven kids dragged the stones thither with all speed, ug put as many of them into iyang stomach as sila could get in; ug the mama sewed him up again in the greatest haste, so that siya was not aware of anything ug never once stirred.

When the wolf at length had had iyang sleep out, siya got on iyang legs, ug as the stones in iyang stomach made him very thirsty, siya gusto moadto to a well to inom. Pero when siya began to walk ug move about, the stones in iyang stomach knocked against each other ug rattled. Unya cried siya,

“What rumbles ug tumbles
Against my poor bones?
Ako thought ’twas six kids,
Pero it’s naught but dako stones.”

Ug when siya got to the well ug stooped over the tubig ug was just about to inom, the heavy stones made him fall in, ug there was wala help, pero siya had to drown miserably. When the seven kids saw that, sila came running to the spot ug cried aloud, “The wolf is dead! The wolf is dead!” ug danced for joy round about the well with their mama.

## Notes on the weaving

The new words:

- **Iyang** is his and her alike, as *siya* is he and she; it stands before the thing owned, as in English: *iyang rough voice*, *iyang children*. *Her* and *him* after a verb or a small word ("called tanan seven to her", "made him thirsty") stay English, and so do *its, their, my, our, your, thy*.
- **Niingon** is said. "Niingon siya" for "said she" happens to be the usual Cebuano order. "Tanan siya said was true" keeps *said*: the thing said is in front there, and Cebuano uses another form of the word for that.
- **Adto** is go, to a place away from here. It is the first word in these versions that shows time by its own beginning: *mo-adto* for going not yet done (*gusto moadto*, "ako have to moadto"), *ni-adto* for done ("the wretch niadto"), and bare *adto* as an order ("Now adto ug look"). In "gusto moadto" the English "to" has been dropped, twice: Cebuano puts the two words straight together. "Go away", "went away", "went out" and "went on iyang way" stay English; they are about leaving, not about going somewhere.
- **Usa** is one, in counting: *usa day*, *usa cut*, *usa gamay kid*. "The old one", "no one", "the only one" and "one after another" keep *one*; they do not count anything.
- **Inom, tulog** are bare roots here, after "to" and "still"; in a Cebuano sentence they would carry a beginning like *adto* does. "Had had iyang sleep out" keeps *sleep*.
- **Kamo** is you to several persons (the mother to her kids), beside *ikaw* to one. **Kita** is we with the listener counted in (the mother to her kids: "kita will fill"), beside *kami* without. "Devour you tanan" and "each of you" keep *you*; after a verb or a small word the form is another one.

The notes on the earlier words are in [the 21-word version](wolf-and-seven-kids.21-words.md).

**Where the words come from.** *Niingon* and *usa* are Tier 2, like *pero* and *tanan*; the other twenty-seven are Tier 1 (*iyang* as a form of *siya*; it has not appeared in the numbered texts yet).
