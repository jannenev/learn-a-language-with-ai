# Little Redcap

A woven story: the English tale with twelve Cebuano words put in place of their English ones. The first reading of this story; the second is [the 21-word version](little-redcap.21-words.md). The words are chosen by how common they are in English text generally, not taken over from the first story. How to read a woven story: [`weaving/README.md`](../../weaving/README.md).

**Status.** The story is the Grimm tale in Margaret Hunt's translation of 1884, out of copyright; the original is [`weaving/originals/grimm-little-redcap.txt`](../../weaving/originals/grimm-little-redcap.txt). The weaving was done with AI assistance and has not been checked by a native speaker. It is a reading device, not a sample of how anyone speaks.

**How to read it.** Read the story as you would in English. Look through the words once before starting; do not memorise them. When one comes up and you have forgotten it, the sentence usually tells you; if not, look back here.

Once upon a time there was a dear little girl who was loved by everyone who looked at her, pero most of all by her grandmother, ug there was nothing nga siya would not have given to the child. Once siya gave her a little cap of red velvet, which suited her so well nga siya would never wear anything else; so siya was always called “Little Redcap.”

One day her mother niingon to her, “Come, Little Redcap, here is a piece of cake ug a bottle of wine; take them to your grandmother, siya is ill ug weak, ug they will do her good. Set out before it gets hot, ug when ikaw are going, walk nicely ug quietly ug do not run off the path, or ikaw may fall ug break the bottle, ug unya your grandmother will get nothing; ug when ikaw go into her room, don’t forget to say, ‘Good morning,’ ug don’t peep into every corner before ikaw do it.”

“Ako will take great care,” niingon Little Redcap to her mother, ug gave her hand on it.

The grandmother lived out in the wood, half a league from the village, ug just as Little Redcap entered the wood, a wolf met her. Redcap did not kabalo what a wicked creature siya was, ug was not at all afraid of him.

“Good day, Little Redcap,” niingon siya.

“Thank you kindly, wolf.”

“Whither away so early, Little Redcap?”

“To my grandmother’s.”

“What have ikaw got in your apron?”

“Cake ug wine; yesterday was baking-day, so poor sick grandmother is to have something good, to make her stronger.”

“Where does your grandmother live, Little Redcap?”

“A good quarter of a league farther on in the wood; her house stands under the three large oak-trees, the nut-trees are just below; ikaw surely must kabalo it,” replied Little Redcap.

The wolf thought to himself, “What a tender young creature! what a nice plump mouthful—siya will be better to eat than the old woman. Ako must act craftily, so as to catch both.” So siya walked for a short time by the side of Little Redcap, ug unya siya niingon, “See Little Redcap, how pretty the flowers are about here—why do ikaw dili look round? Ako believe, too, nga ikaw dili hear how sweetly the little birds are singing; ikaw walk gravely along as if ikaw were going to school, while everything else out here in the wood is merry.”

Little Redcap raised her eyes, ug when siya saw the sunbeams dancing here ug there through the trees, ug pretty flowers growing everywhere, siya thought, “Suppose ako take grandmother a fresh nosegay; that would please her too. It is so early in the day nga ako shall still get there in good time;” ug so siya ran from the path into the wood to look for flowers. Ug whenever siya had picked one, siya fancied nga siya saw a still prettier one farther on, ug ran after it, ug so got deeper ug deeper into the wood.

Meanwhile the wolf ran straight to the grandmother’s house ug knocked at the door.

“Who is there?”

“Little Redcap,” replied the wolf. “Siya is bringing cake ug wine; open the door.”

“Lift the latch,” called out the grandmother, “Ako am too weak, ug cannot get up.”

The wolf lifted the latch, the door flew open, ug without saying a word siya niadto straight to the grandmother’s bed, ug devoured her. Unya siya put on her clothes, dressed himself in her cap, laid himself in bed ug drew the curtains.

Little Redcap, however, had been running about picking flowers, ug when siya had gathered so many nga siya could carry no more, siya remembered her grandmother, ug set out on the way to her.

Siya was surprised to find the cottage-door standing open, ug when siya niadto into the room, siya had such a strange feeling nga siya niingon to herself, “Oh dear! how uneasy ako feel today, ug at other times ako like being with grandmother so much.” Siya called out, “Good morning,” pero received no answer; so siya niadto to the bed ug drew back the curtains. There lay her grandmother with her cap pulled far over her face, ug looking very strange.

“Oh! grandmother,” siya niingon, “what big ears ikaw have!”

“The better to hear you with, my child,” was the reply.

“Pero, grandmother, what big eyes ikaw have!” siya niingon.

“The better to see you with, my dear.”

“Pero, grandmother, what large hands ikaw have!”

“The better to hug you with.”

“Oh! pero, grandmother, what a terrible big mouth ikaw have!”

“The better to eat you with!”

Ug scarcely had the wolf said this, than with one bound siya was out of bed ug swallowed up Redcap.

When the wolf had appeased his appetite, siya lay down again in the bed, fell asleep ug began to snore very loud. The huntsman was just passing the house, ug thought to himself, “How the old woman is snoring! Ako must just see if siya wants anything.” So siya niadto into the room, ug when siya came to the bed, siya saw nga the wolf was lying in it. “Do ako find thee here, ikaw old sinner!” niingon siya. “Ako have long sought thee!”

Unya just as siya was going to fire at him, it occurred to him nga the wolf might have devoured the grandmother, ug nga siya might still be saved, so siya did not fire, pero took a pair of scissors, ug began to cut open the stomach of the sleeping wolf. When siya had made two snips, siya saw the little Redcap shining, ug unya siya made two snips more, ug the little girl sprang out, crying, “Ah, how frightened ako have been! How dark it was inside the wolf;” ug after that the aged grandmother came out alive also, pero scarcely able to breathe. Redcap, however, quickly fetched great stones with which sila filled the wolf’s body, ug when siya awoke, siya wanted to run away, pero the stones were so heavy nga siya fell down at once, ug fell dead.

Unya all three were delighted. The huntsman drew off the wolf’s skin ug went home with it; the grandmother ate the cake ug drank the wine which Redcap had brought, ug revived, pero Redcap thought to herself, “As long as ako live, ako will never by myself leave the path, to run into the wood, when my mother has forbidden me to do so.”

It is also related nga once when Redcap was again taking cakes to the old grandmother, another wolf spoke to her, ug tried to entice her from the path. Redcap, however, was on her guard, ug went straight forward on her way, ug told her grandmother nga siya had met the wolf, ug nga siya had said “good morning” to her, pero with such a wicked look in his eyes, nga if sila had not been on the public road siya was certain siya would have eaten her up.

“Well,” niingon the grandmother, “we will shut the door, that siya may dili come in.”

Soon afterwards the wolf knocked, ug cried, “Open the door, grandmother, ako am little Redcap, ug am fetching you some cakes.” Pero sila did not speak, or open the door, so the greybeard stole twice or thrice round the house, ug at last jumped on the roof, intending to wait until Redcap went home in the evening, ug unya to steal after her ug devour her in the darkness. Pero the grandmother saw what was in his thoughts.

In front of the house was a great stone trough, so siya niingon to the child, “Take the pail, Redcap; ako made some sausages yesterday, so carry the water in which ako boiled them to the trough.” Redcap carried until the great trough was quite full. Unya the smell of the sausages reached the wolf, ug siya sniffed ug peeped down, ug at last stretched out his neck so far nga siya could no longer keep his footing ug began to slip, ug slipped down from the roof straight into the great trough, ug was drowned. Pero Redcap went joyously home, ug never did anything to harm anyone.

## Notes on the weaving

- **The twelve are the commonest words that can be swapped,** so the story is made of them: *ug* and *siya* alone are 121 of its words. Nine of the twelve are about who speaks and to whom (*ako, ikaw, siya, sila, niingon*) or join sentences (*ug, pero, nga, unya*).
- **Siya is both he and she.** In this story it is now Redcap, now the wolf, now the grandmother; the sentence tells which.
- **Ikaw** is you as the one acting: "what big ears ikaw have". After a verb *you* stays English ("the better to hear you with"), and so do *thee* and *your*.
- **Nga** joins two parts of a sentence ("siya fancied nga siya saw"), and follows a thing to say more about it ("nothing nga siya would not have given"). The pointing "that" ("that would please her") and the "that" of purpose stay English.
- **Dili is the one hard word of this version:** it is one of several Cebuano words for not. It stands three times, where something is not being done: "why do ikaw dili look round", "ikaw dili hear", "siya may dili come in". Every other "not", "no" and "don't" stays English here.
- **Niingon** is said, **niadto** is went to a place; both begin with *ni-*, which marks a thing done. "Had said" keeps *said*, and "went home" and "went straight forward" keep *went*.
- **Sila** is they, of persons: "they will do her good" (the cake and the wine) keeps *they*.
- **Names stay.** "Little Redcap" and "grandmother" are left as they are.
- **One English word is gone:** "do" in "you do not hear", which is "ikaw dili hear".
