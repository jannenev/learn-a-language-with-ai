There was once upon a time an old goat who had seven gamay kids, ug loved them with all the love of a mama for her children. One day siya gusto to go into the forest ug fetch some food. So siya called all seven to her ug said, “Dear children, I have to go into the forest, be on your guard against the wolf; if siya come in, siya will devour you all—skin, hair, ug all. The wretch often disguises himself, but you will know him at once by his rough voice ug his black feet.”

The kids said, “Dear mama, we will take good care of ourselves; you may go away without any anxiety.” Unya the old one bleated, ug went on her way with an easy mind.

It was dili long before someone knocked at the balay-door ug called, “Open the door, dear children; your mama is here, ug has brought something back with her for each of you.”

But the gamay kids kabalo that it was the wolf, by the rough voice; “We will dili open the door,” cried sila, “thou art dili our mama. Siya has a soft, pleasant voice, but thy voice is rough; thou art the wolf!”

Unya the wolf went away to a shopkeeper ug bought himself a great lump of chalk, ate this ug made his voice soft with it. Unya siya came back, knocked at the door of the balay, ug cried, “Open the door, dear children, your mama is here ug has brought something back with her for each of you.”

But the wolf had laid his black paws against the window, ug the children saw them ug cried, “We will dili open the door, our mama has not black feet like thee; thou art the wolf.”

Unya the wolf ran to a baker ug said, “I have hurt my feet, rub some dough over them for me.” Ug when the baker had rubbed his feet over, siya ran to the miller ug said, “Strew some white meal over my feet for me.”

The miller thought to himself, “The wolf gusto to deceive someone,” ug refused; but the wolf said, “If thou wilt dili do it, I will devour thee.” Unya the miller was afraid, ug made his paws white for him. Truly men are like that.

So now the wretch went for the third time to the balay-door, knocked at it ug said, “Open the door for me, children, your dear little mama has come home, ug has brought every one of you something back from the forest with her.”

The gamay kids cried, “First show us thy paws that we may kabalo if thou art our dear little mama.” Unya siya put his paws in through the window, ug when the kids saw that they were white, sila believed that all siya said was true, ug opened the door. But who should come in but the wolf! Sila were terrified ug gusto to hide themselves. One sprang under the table, the second into the bed, the third into the stove, the fourth into the kitchen, the fifth into the cupboard, the sixth under the washing-bowl, ug the seventh into the clock-case. But the wolf found them all, ug used no great ceremony; one after the other siya swallowed them down his throat. The youngest, who was in the clock-case, was the only one siya did not find. When the wolf had satisfied his appetite siya took himself off, laid himself down under a tree in the green meadow outside, ug began to sleep. Soon afterwards the old goat came home again from the forest. Ah! What a sight siya saw didto! The balay-door stood wide open. The table, chairs, ug benches were thrown down, the washing-bowl lay broken to pieces, ug the quilts ug pillows were pulled off the bed. Siya sought her children, but sila were nowhere to be found. Siya called them one after another by name, but no one answered.

At last, when siya came to the youngest, a soft voice cried, “Dear mama, I am in the clock-case.” Siya took the kid out, ug it told her that the wolf had come ug had eaten all the others. Then you may imagine how siya wept over her poor children.

At length in her grief siya went out, ug the youngest kid ran with her. When sila came to the meadow, didto lay the wolf by the tree ug snored so loud that the branches shook. Siya looked at him on every side ug saw that something was moving ug struggling in his gorged belly. “Ah, heavens,” said siya, “is it possible that my poor children whom siya has swallowed down for his supper, can be still alive?” Unya the kid had to run home ug fetch scissors, ug a needle ug thread, ug the goat cut open the monster’s stomach, ug hardly had siya make one cut, than one gamay kid thrust its head out, ug when siya cut farther, all six sprang out one after another, ug were all still alive, ug had suffered no injury whatever, for in his greediness the monster had swallowed them down whole. What rejoicing there was! Sila embraced their dear mama, ug jumped like a sailor at his wedding. The mama, however, said, “Now go ug look for some dako stones, ug we will fill the wicked beast’s stomach with them while siya is still asleep.” Unya the seven kids dragged the stones thither with all speed, ug put as many of them into his stomach as sila could get in; ug the mama sewed him up again in the greatest haste, so that siya was not aware of anything ug never once stirred.

When the wolf at length had had his sleep out, siya got on his legs, ug as the stones in his stomach made him very thirsty, siya gusto to go to a well to drink. But when siya began to walk ug move about, the stones in his stomach knocked against each other ug rattled. Unya cried siya,

“What rumbles ug tumbles
Against my poor bones?
I thought ’twas six kids,
But it’s naught but dako stones.”

Ug when siya got to the well ug stooped over the water ug was just about to drink, the heavy stones made him fall in, ug there was no help, but siya had to drown miserably. When the seven kids saw that, sila came running to the spot ug cried aloud, “The wolf is dead! The wolf is dead!” ug danced for joy round about the well with their mama.

## Notes on the weaving

- **Siya is both he and she.** Cebuano does not say which. In this story *siya* is now the wolf, now the mother; the sentence around it tells. *His, her, him* and *himself* stay English at this level; they are other forms of the same word.
- **Sila is they, for persons.** "They were white" (the paws) keeps *they*. *Them* and *their* stay English.
- **Dili is one of two words for not.** It is used here where something is refused or denied: "we will dili open", "thou art dili our mama", "it was dili long". Three places keep *not* ("has not black feet", "did not find", "was not aware"): there Cebuano uses its other word, *wala*, which comes at a later level.
- **Unya is then, the next thing that happens.** "Then you may imagine how she wept" keeps *then*; it is not a next event.
- **Other words left in English on purpose.** "You will know him by his rough voice" keeps *know*: knowing a person is another Cebuano word, not *kabalo*. "Dear little mother" keeps *little*: it is a pet name there, not a size.
- **Word forms.** *Gusto* stands for want, wants and wanted alike, and *kabalo* for know and knew; these Cebuano words do not change for time.
- **Mama.** The everyday word, also used as a noun; the formal word for mother is not in the course's lists.
- **Where the words come from.** All twelve are Tier 1. By the text where each first appears: 001 *gusto*; 002 *kabalo, balay, mama, unya, didto*; 003 *dili*; 004 *siya, dako, gamay*; 005 *sila, ug*.
